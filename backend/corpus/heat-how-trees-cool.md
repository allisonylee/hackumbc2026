**Key takeaway:** Trees cool streets in two ways: their leaves block sunlight (shade), and they release water vapor that cools the air (evapotranspiration). EPA cites a review of 308 studies finding urban forests were on average 3.0°F (1.6°C) cooler than urban areas without greenery.

## Shade
A tree's canopy stops sunlight before it reaches pavement, walls and roofs. Shaded surfaces stay cooler, so they give off less heat to the air and to the people walking or sitting nearby. Shade over a rowhouse can also keep the inside cooler.

## Evapotranspiration
Trees pull water up through their roots and let it evaporate from their leaves. Turning water into vapor uses heat energy, which cools the surrounding air. This works a bit like sweat cooling your skin. Trees also cool the air when water evaporates from the soil around them and from rain caught on their leaves.

## Other benefits EPA lists
- **Lower energy use:** Parks and urban trees can cut the energy demand of nearby buildings by about **10%**, which also cuts greenhouse gas emissions from power plants.
- **Cleaner air:** Tall, dense roadside vegetation can reduce pollution downwind by about **30%**.
- **Less flooding:** Urban trees can absorb **15 to 27%** of yearly rainfall, reducing stormwater runoff.
- **Fewer heat deaths:** One analysis estimated that a **10%** increase in tree cover would mean about **50** fewer deaths a year in Salt Lake City and **3,800** fewer in New York City.
- **Wildlife:** Trees give homes and food to pollinators, birds and small mammals.
- **Fairness:** Adding vegetation in underserved neighborhoods helps communities that face more than their share of heat.

## What this means for Baltimore
Blocks with lots of pavement and few trees miss out on both cooling effects. Planting street trees where shade is scarce is one of the most direct ways to bring these benefits to the people who need them most.
