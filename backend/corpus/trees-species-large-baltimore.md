**Key takeaway:** Baltimore prefers large shade trees wherever there is room, because they give more benefits and live longer. Blackgum, willow oak and swamp white oak are native large street trees on the current list. London planetree is approved but nonnative. For Kentucky coffeetree, only a fruitless cultivar is approved for streets. **Red maple needs special Forestry approval** because it is overplanted.

## How the city defines "large"
**Large trees:** over 50 feet tall at maturity (height varies with site). "Native" on this list means historically present in the Mid-Atlantic region.

## Blackgum (Nyssa sylvatica)
Approved large street tree. Native. A relative, swamp tupelo (Nyssa biflora), is also approved, native, and marked as expected to do better as the climate warms.

## Willow oak (Quercus phellos)
Approved large street tree. Native.

## Swamp white oak (Quercus bicolor)
Approved large street tree. Native. It carries a minus (–) climate rating, meaning it is projected to do *worse* under future warming.

## London planetree (Platanus × acerifolia)
Approved large street tree. **Nonnative.** The list names it Platanus × hispanica, with Platanus × acerifolia as a synonym. Its native relative, American sycamore, should be avoided in highly urbanized areas because of the disease anthracnose.

## Kentucky coffeetree (Gymnocladus dioicus)
Native. For streets, the approved form is the fruitless 'Espresso-JFS' cultivar. The regular species is approved for wide medians (15 feet or wider), with the note that female trees drop pods.

## Red maple (Acer rubrum)
Native, but in the "Special Forestry Approval Required" section: **overplanted, do not plant without specific Forestry approval**. The same applies to the Armstrong and Autumn Blaze hybrid maples.

## Other native large street trees on the list
Examples: thornless honeylocust, seedless sweetgum, white oak, southern red oak, Shumard oak, bald cypress, American linden and Jefferson elm.

Before planting any tree in the public right-of-way, you need permission from the Baltimore City Forestry Division.
