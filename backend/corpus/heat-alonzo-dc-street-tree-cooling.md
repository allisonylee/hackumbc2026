**Key takeaway:** In Washington, DC, tree canopy cooled afternoon air by about 1.8°C (3.24°F), but the cooling depended on what was under the trees and the time of day. Trees over soil and grass cooled much more than trees over pavement, and spread-out street trees were especially useful in the evening.

## The study
Michael Alonzo, Matthew Baker, Yuemeng Gao and Vivek Shandas used nine cars fitted with temperature sensors to drive across Washington, DC, on August 28, 2018. They collected more than 70,000 air temperature readings at three times: before dawn (5–6 a.m.), afternoon (2–3 p.m.) and evening (6–7 p.m.). They split tree canopy into "soft" canopy (over unpaved ground) and "hard" canopy (over pavement), and into clustered patches versus trees spread out along streets. It was published in Environmental Research Letters on August 4, 2021. This is a single-city field study, not a review of many studies.

## What they found
- **Overall canopy cooling:** about **1.8°C** in the afternoon, **1.7°C** in the evening and **0.5°C** before dawn.
- **Soft canopy (trees over soil):** about **2.0°C** of afternoon cooling, with little effect until cover reached roughly **40%**; about **2.9°C** of evening cooling that rose steadily with more cover.
- **Hard canopy (trees over pavement):** much smaller cooling effects.
- **Patches vs. spread-out trees:** Clustered patches cooled consistently. Spread-out trees cooled before dawn and in the evening, when the sun is lower and shadows are longer. The authors note that scattered trees give more shade per unit of canopy than a forest patch.

## What it means for Baltimore
Baltimore is nearby and has a similar summer climate. The results suggest that trees with more unpaved ground beneath them, and trees spread along blocks, may do more cooling per tree. It also points to about 40% canopy as a level where daytime cooling picks up.
