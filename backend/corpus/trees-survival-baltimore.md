**Key takeaway:** A US Forest Service study of Baltimore found that about **6.6 percent** of the city's trees died each year. The smallest and youngest trees died fastest, and trees along roads died fastest of all. Cutting deaths even a little makes a big difference to how much canopy the city keeps, which is why caring for newly planted trees matters so much.

## What the Baltimore study found
Researchers measured randomly placed plots across all land uses in Baltimore in 1999 and again in 2001.
- **Overall:** 6.6 percent of trees died per year. The number of live trees fell by 4.2 percent per year after counting new trees.
- **By land use:** yearly mortality was 20.2 percent in transportation areas (roads and rights-of-way), 10.6 percent in commercial/industrial areas, and 6.0 percent in high-density residential areas. It was only 2.2 percent in low- to medium-density residential areas.
- **By size:** the smallest trees (0–7.6 cm trunk diameter) had the highest yearly mortality at 9.0 percent. Mid-size trees (30.6–45.7 cm) had the lowest at 0.5 percent.
- **Life span:** at these rates, the average Baltimore tree would live about **15 years**, and only 30 percent would live past age 15.

## Why lowering mortality matters
In the model, cutting the average yearly death rate from 6.6 to 2.3 percent left about 1,988,000 trees after 100 years instead of 183,000. Raising it to 11.6 percent left only about 1,000. Even if recent levels of new tree planting continued, the study projected Baltimore's canopy would decline by up to 12 percent over the next 100 years.

## What earlier studies (cited in the paper) found about new plantings
- In Boston, newly planted street trees died at rates from 3 percent to more than 38 percent, depending on the planting contractor.
- In Oakland, California, inner-city street trees planted **with community involvement** had about 60–70 percent survival. Those planted without it had less than 1 percent survival.
- In northern England, the main causes of death for new trees were water and nutrient stress (56 percent), vandalism (18 percent), girdling by tree guards (12 percent), soil compaction (9 percent) and poor staking (5 percent).

## The lesson
Young trees are most likely to die before they are established. Steady watering, protection from damage and neighbor involvement in the first few years give a new street tree its best chance of growing large enough to give real shade.
