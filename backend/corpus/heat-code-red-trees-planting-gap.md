**Key takeaway:** Baltimore's official goal is 40% tree canopy by 2037. The 2019 Code Red investigation reported that the city and nonprofits would need to plant about 25,000 trees a year to get there, but were planting about 10,000 a year. The poorest, hottest neighborhoods, such as Broadway East, have the least canopy.

## About the story
"No trees, no shade, no relief as climate heats up" was published September 3, 2019, by the University of Maryland's Howard Center for Investigative Journalism and Capital News Service. It was reported by Xander Ready, Theresa Diffendal, Bryan Gallion and Sean Mussenden.

## Where Baltimore's canopy stands
- Baltimore's tree canopy was **28%** in 2015, up from **27%** in 2007, according to U.S. Forest Service researchers. The city's forestry division estimated it was still around 28% in 2019.
- About **40%** of city neighborhoods had a net loss of canopy over that period.

## The planting gap
To reach **40%** canopy by **2037**, the city and its partners would need to plant about **25,000** trees each year, up from roughly **10,000** a year. The city arborist warned that pace would miss the goal.

## Unequal shade
- The coolest Baltimore neighborhood has **10 times** more tree canopy than the hottest.
- Several poor areas had less than **10%** canopy, while several wealthier areas had more than **40%**.
- **Broadway East**, one of the poorest and hottest parts of the city, has about **10%** canopy, six times less than tree-lined **Roland Park**, where about two-thirds of the neighborhood is under tree canopy. Between 2007 and 2015 Broadway East grew from **9%** to **10.6%**, while Roland Park grew by **2.1** percentage points.
- Canopy follows 1930s "redlining" maps: neighborhoods rated "best" average about **45%** canopy today, while redlined neighborhoods average about **11%**.
- Fewer than half of larger street trees (trunks over 6 inches) in Broadway East were rated "good" in the city's street tree census, compared with about **75%** in Roland Park.

## Why planting is hard
New street trees need about **20 gallons** of water a week for their first two years. Groups like the Baltimore Tree Trust, a nonprofit founded in 2008, plant and water trees in some of the hottest neighborhoods, including Broadway East. Young trees give little shade for years. On many blocks, sidewalks are too narrow to fit a good-sized tree and still leave room for wheelchairs under the Americans with Disabilities Act. Planting often means breaking concrete, and large roots can crack and lift sidewalks.

## The 40% link
The story cites a 2019 University of Wisconsin and Concordia University study: blocks needed at least 40% canopy for the biggest temperature drops.
