**Key takeaway:** Where trees are planted matters as much as how many. This study built a math tool (linear programming optimization) that picks planting locations to balance cost, cooling, clean air, stormwater and fairness. Different goals led to very different planting plans, showing that equity has to be built in on purpose.

## The study
"A multi-objective decision support framework to prioritize tree planting locations in urban areas," by Charity Nyelele and Charles Kroll (SUNY College of Environmental Science and Forestry), was a case study of the Bronx, New York. It covered 1,132 census block groups and chose how much tree cover to add on two kinds of plantable land in each: pervious areas (grass or bare soil) and impervious areas (pavement).

## What it weighed
- Increase in tree cover
- Money saved from avoided stormwater runoff
- Fine particle (PM2.5) air pollution removed
- Heat index reduction
- Planting cost: they assumed $430 per square meter on pavement (about $2,150 per tree) and $100 per square meter on pervious ground (about $500 per tree)
- Fairness, measured two ways: **equality** (how evenly canopy is spread, using the Gini coefficient, where 0 is perfectly even) and **equity** (whether disadvantaged groups, such as people below the poverty line or Black residents, get more benefit)

They tested 14 scenarios. Several used the $400 million MillionTreesNYC budget as a spending limit.

## Findings
- The Bronx's 2010 baseline had a canopy Gini of 0.35. Some scenarios reduced this inequality; one kept the Gini below 0.25 while maximizing canopy.
- Adding a goal to target people below the poverty line gave the highest resulting canopy (25.9%) among the canopy-maximizing scenarios.
- There were trade-offs: pushing for a more equal spread of trees meant giving up some air-pollution benefit, and more planting on costly paved sites.
- Targeting populations directly steered trees away from large block groups that are mostly parks with few residents.

## Why it matters
The authors warn that planting only for one goal can make inequity worse. They conclude that multi-objective tools can help cities and community groups get more total benefit from greening while meeting equity goals.
