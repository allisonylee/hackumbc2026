**Key takeaway:** In Baltimore, how much of an area is covered by trees was a better predictor of surface temperature than how those trees were arranged. Trees also cooled more efficiently in Baltimore than in hotter, drier Sacramento. For Baltimore, the simple message is: more canopy means cooler ground.

## The study
Weiqi Zhou, Jia Wang and Mary Cadenasso compared Baltimore, Maryland, with Sacramento, California, two cities with very different climates. They mapped trees from 1-meter resolution imagery and compared tree patterns with land surface temperature, the heat of the ground and rooftops measured by satellite. They repeated the analysis with different statistical methods and with study areas of different sizes. The paper appeared in Remote Sensing of Environment, volume 195 (2017).

## What they found
- **Trees cooled more in Baltimore.** Their cooling efficiency was higher in Baltimore than in Sacramento.
- **Amount beats arrangement in Baltimore.** Percent tree cover was more important than spatial configuration (the size, shape and spacing of tree patches) for predicting temperature in Baltimore. In Sacramento it was the reverse.
- **Arrangement effects flip between cities.** In Baltimore, bigger average tree patches were linked to *warmer* surfaces once tree amount was accounted for, and more patch edges were linked to cooler surfaces. Sacramento showed the opposite pattern.
- **Method matters.** Results about arrangement changed a lot depending on the statistics used, and relationships got stronger as the analysis areas got larger. The authors stress controlling for the amount of tree cover before drawing conclusions about arrangement.

## What it means for planting
For Baltimore, raising the overall share of tree canopy is the main lever for cooling. Details of how trees are grouped matter less here than simply adding more canopy.
