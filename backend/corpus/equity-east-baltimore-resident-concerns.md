**Key takeaway:** When researchers asked East Baltimore residents about tree planting, opinions were split. Many valued shade and beauty, but others raised real concerns, like dead trees the city never removed, pests, property damage, and distrust of outside plans. The authors say those concerns must be heard, not dismissed.

## The study
"It's Not Easy Going Green: Obstacles to Tree-Planting Programs in East Baltimore" looked at two neighborhoods, Berea and Madison-Eastend. Researchers found enough plantable space to raise tree canopy from about six percent to over 16 percent. They also interviewed 26 residents in December 2009 and April 2010; all were African American adults. East Baltimore was chosen partly because the city's forestry division met resistance to planting there in the 1960s.

## What supporters said
14 of the 26 people had something positive to say. Shade was the most common reason: Baltimore summers can reach 100 °F, not everyone has air conditioning, and people sit out on stoops and pick the shady side of the street. Residents also said trees make a block look better, and a few mentioned lower energy bills or cleaner air.

## What worried people
The other 12 opposed more planting. Their reasons included:
- **Dead and neglected trees.** Several asked why the city would plant new trees when it hadn't removed dead ones they had reported, or when earlier plantings had died.
- **Pests and mess:** bird droppings, caterpillars, and a belief that trees attract rats.
- **Allergies** to pollen.
- **Property damage:** roots in water pipes, and branches hitting wires and vacant houses.
- **Crime:** some said trees could be used to hide drugs.
- **Distrust and gentrification.** One resident feared planting would be followed by displacement, pointing to blocks cleared nearby for hospital expansion. Another saw it as a politician's "flavor of the week."
- **Other priorities,** such as trash and dirty streets.
- **Who will water them?** Homeowners had to sign a waiver agreeing to water young trees, and some doubted neighbors, especially renters, would.

## Lessons
The authors found that views came from people's practical, lived experience, not from their ethnic group. They suggest the city (1) take care of existing trees, including removing dead and hazardous ones, and (2) involve residents in decisions, which could also help restore trust in city government. Talking about cooling and energy savings may work better than talking about property values.
