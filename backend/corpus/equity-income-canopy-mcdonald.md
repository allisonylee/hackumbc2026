---
title: "Do lower-income neighborhoods have fewer trees and more heat? (McDonald et al. 2021)"
url: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0249715
source: McDonald et al., PLOS ONE, 2021
---

**Key takeaway:** In almost every large US urban area, lower-income neighborhoods have less tree cover and are hotter in summer than higher-income neighborhoods. On average, low-income blocks have 15.2% less tree cover and are 1.5 °C (2.7 °F) hotter.

## What the study looked at
The paper is "The tree cover and temperature disparity in US urbanized areas: Quantifying the association with income across 5,723 communities." The authors studied 5,723 communities (incorporated places and census-designated places) inside 100 US urbanized areas. They compared tree cover and summer surface temperature between low-income and high-income census blocks.

## Main findings
- Low-income blocks had 15.2% less tree cover than high-income blocks on average.
- Low-income blocks were 1.5 °C (2.7 °F) hotter in summer surface temperature.
- This pattern showed up in 92% of the urbanized areas studied, so it is close to universal.
- The gap was largest in the Northeast, where in some areas low-income blocks had 30% less tree cover and were 4.0 °C (7.2 °F) hotter.

## The size of the gap
- Low-income blocks have about 62 million fewer trees than high-income blocks.
- The authors value that missing tree cover at about $56 billion, or $1,349 per person.
- Closing the gap would take an estimated $17.6 billion in tree planting and natural regeneration.
- About 42 million people living in low-income blocks would benefit.

## What it means
Trees shade streets and cool neighborhoods, so a shortage of trees adds to heat stress for the people who often have the fewest ways to escape it. The study summary does not give numbers for Baltimore specifically. Planting programs that focus on low-income, low-canopy blocks can help narrow this gap.
