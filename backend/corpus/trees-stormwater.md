**Key takeaway:** Tree crowns act like small reservoirs. Leaves and bark catch rain, and some of it evaporates before it ever reaches the pavement. This means less runoff, a delayed peak flow, and less polluted water washing into streams. It matters most in small storms, which carry most of the pollution.

## How it works
The US Forest Service Northeast guide describes three paths for rain that hits a tree:
- **Interception:** water held on leaves and branches, then evaporated. This water never becomes runoff.
- **Stemflow:** water that runs down branches and the trunk to the ground.
- **Throughfall:** water that drips off leaves or falls through gaps.

Roots also draw water out of the soil (transpiration), which leaves the soil able to soak up more water in the next storm.

## How much rain a tree catches
- A tree crown can hold only about 50 to 100 gallons during a big storm. After that, extra rain passes through. So trees help most with water quality in small, frequent storms. They help less with flood control in large storms.
- In the guide's model, averaged over 40 years, a small tree intercepted about 358 gal/year, a medium red maple 1,156 gal/year, a large zelkova 1,909 gal/year and an eastern white pine 909 gal/year.
- Studies that simulated whole urban forests found runoff reductions of 2 to 7 percent.

## What makes a tree better at catching rain
- Bigger trees, because they have more surface area.
- Rough leaf and bark surfaces, which hold more water than smooth ones.
- Dense crowns with few gaps.
- Being in leaf when rain is heaviest. Conifers and evergreens catch more in seasons when deciduous trees are bare.

## Tips from the guide
- Plant large trees where possible.
- Don't pave over planting strips beside the street. Paving harms trees and increases runoff.
- In roadside swales and other bioretention areas, pick long-lived, wide-spreading species that tolerate flooding.

The guide values this benefit using what communities pay to control runoff. For example, at $0.01 per gallon, a tree that catches 1,000 gallons is worth about $10 per year for stormwater alone.
