**Key takeaway:** Across 108 US urban areas, neighborhoods that were "redlined" in the 1930s are hotter today than neighborhoods that got the best grades. Baltimore stood out: its formerly redlined areas were among the hottest in the study compared with the rest of the city.

## What the study did
The paper, "The Effects of Historical Housing Policies on Resident Exposure to Intra-Urban Heat: A Study of 108 US Urban Areas," compared old Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC) maps with summer land surface temperatures measured by the Landsat 8 satellite. Land surface temperature is how hot the ground and roofs are, not the air temperature.

HOLC drew color-coded maps of 239 US cities with populations over 40,000. Each area got one of four grades: A "Best" (green), B "Still Desirable" (blue), C "Definitely Declining" (yellow), and D "Hazardous" (red). The grades were largely based on who lived there, especially race, and affected who could get a mortgage. D areas are the ones called "redlined."

## Main findings
- About 94% of the urban areas studied had warmer surface temperatures in D-rated areas than in A-rated areas.
- Nationally, D-rated areas were on average 2.6 °C (4.68 °F) warmer than A-rated areas. Each worse grade was warmer than the grade above it.
- The gap between D and A areas within one city ranged from +7.1 °C (Portland, OR) to −1.5 °C (Joliet, IL). Some Midwest cities showed the reverse pattern.
- Southeast and Western cities showed the biggest differences; Midwest cities the smallest.

## Baltimore
The authors report that the warmest formerly redlined areas, relative to their own city's average, were in Chattanooga, TN (3.3 °C) and **Baltimore, MD (3.2 °C, or 5.76 °F)** above the citywide average surface temperature.

## Why
Redlined areas today tend to have less tree canopy and more pavement and other impervious surfaces, which trap heat. The authors say this explains part of the pattern but other factors may also play a role. They argue that historical housing policies may be directly responsible for unequal heat exposure today. Redlining was banned by the Fair Housing Act of 1968, but many formerly redlined areas are still mostly lower-income communities of color.
