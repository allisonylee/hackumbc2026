title: "Why tree planting should start by asking residents"

**Key takeaway:** Having space to plant trees is not enough. A tree program only works over the long run if the people who live on the block want the trees and trust that they will be cared for. Researchers who studied East Baltimore concluded that the city's challenge is to win residents' support and respond to their priorities, not just to fill empty tree pits.

## "Possible" vs. "preferable" canopy
The study separates two ideas. **Possible** tree canopy is where it is physically possible to plant. **Preferable** tree canopy is where it is socially desirable to plant: where trees are needed and wanted by residents. In Berea and Madison-Eastend there was room to grow canopy from about six percent to over 16 percent, but interviews showed mixed feelings: 14 of 26 residents supported planting and 12 opposed it.

## Lessons from Baltimore's history
In the 1950s and 1960s, many East Baltimore residents found street trees undesirable, and in the 1960s some resisted the city's planting effort. Fifty years later many blocks were still nearly treeless. The authors note that later residents inherited that bare landscape during a time of disinvestment, so adding canopy now takes a large investment. They also warn against assuming what a racial or ethnic group thinks about trees; people's views came from their own practical experience.

## What the authors recommend
1. **Take care of the trees already there.** Residents were frustrated by dead, dying and hazardous trees that were not removed. Meeting that need could build support for new planting.
2. **Bring residents into decisions.** New ways to engage and empower neighborhoods could ease negative views of trees and help rebuild trust in city government.
3. **Speak to local concerns.** Education should fit each neighborhood. Stressing cooler streets and lower energy bills is more likely to win support than stressing property values or wildlife.
4. **Don't overload residents.** Expecting low-income residents, especially renters, to water and care for trees can be an unfair burden, so long-term care needs a plan.

## Why it matters
Planting in low-canopy neighborhoods can close the tree equity gap, but trees that residents didn't want, or that die from neglect, don't cool anyone. Asking first, and following through on maintenance, is part of doing tree equity right.
