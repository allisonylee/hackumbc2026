**Key takeaway:** In 1937 the federal Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC) made a color-coded map of Baltimore that graded neighborhoods for mortgage lending. Neighborhoods where Black residents lived were marked red ("redlined") even when they were solidly middle class, which cut them off from home loans and helped create wealth and neighborhood gaps that are still visible today.

## How the grades worked
HOLC maps colored neighborhoods green, blue, yellow or red. The page describes greenlined areas as eligible for loans backed by the Federal Housing Administration (FHA), while redlined and yellowlined areas were not. In places like Sandtown and Winchester, the FHA and private lenders refused to back mortgages, or lent only at very high rates.

According to The Baltimore Story, the color system was shaped by eugenics and biased research about race. Areas with Black residents were colored red regardless of their actual economic condition, while areas with residents of English, German, Scottish, Irish and Scandinavian background were far more likely to be greenlined.

## Baltimore examples
- **Homeland** and **Roland Park**, economically successful white neighborhoods, were marked green and blue.
- **Sandtown** and **Winchester**, African American neighborhoods, were marked yellow and red.

## Background and legacy
The page describes Baltimore's 1911 Ordinance 610 as a forerunner of the redlining that spread across the country in the 1930s. It also quotes the US Commission on Civil Rights, which in 1973 blamed the housing industry and government together for creating segregated housing.

Many white families built wealth through home equity, which they could pass to their children or use to start businesses and pay for school. People of color confined to redlined neighborhoods were largely shut out of this. The page notes that a map of poverty in Baltimore today lines up almost exactly with the 1937 HOLC map: areas once marked yellow or red still struggle, while many green and blue areas thrive.

You can explore the original map through the Mapping Inequality project. Research also links redlined areas to hotter temperatures and fewer trees today (see the Hoffman et al. 2020 document).
