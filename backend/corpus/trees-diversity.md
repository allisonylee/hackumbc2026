**Key takeaway:** When a city plants too much of one kind of tree, a single pest or disease can kill a large share of its canopy at once. Emerald ash borer showed this: cities where ash made up about 20 percent of trees lost roughly one in five trees. Spreading plantings across many species, genera and families is the best protection.

## The old rule and why it fell short
The Wisconsin DNR calls the long-used "30-20-10" planting rule out of date, because it was not adequate to handle the emerald ash borer epidemic. Losing one in five trees was too much for city budgets, environmental benefits and public support.

## A stricter guideline: 20-10-5
The DNR suggests planting no more than:
- **20 percent** from one **family**. Example: the soapberry family (Sapindaceae) includes maples, horsechestnuts and buckeyes, all counted together.
- **10 percent** from one **genus**. Example: all maples (Acer) together, including red, sugar, silver, Norway and Japanese maples and box elder.
- **5 percent** from one **species**, counting all its cultivars. Example: named varieties like 'Crimson King' or 'Emerald Queen' are all still Norway maple.

Some experts suggest keeping any one genus as low as 5 percent. That has to be balanced against what suits the site and what nurseries can supply.

## Diversity on each street, and across ages
- Diversity is also about **place**. If one street or neighborhood is planted with a single species or genus, the citywide risk may look small, but the local loss could be devastating.
- It is also about **age**. A healthy urban forest usually has many more young trees than old ones, kept up by steady planting and replacement. If most trees are the same age, many can fail at about the same time with nothing coming up behind them.
