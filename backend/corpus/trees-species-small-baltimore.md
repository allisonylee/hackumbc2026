**Key takeaway:** Baltimore's current approved list puts **eastern redbud** and **Canadian serviceberry** in the "small" group (under 35 feet at maturity), which the city says suits narrow streets, small tree pits and spots under overhead utility wires. Both are listed as native. **Hedge maple is now on the city's do-not-plant list** because it is invasive.

## How the city defines "small"
- **Small trees:** under 35 feet tall at maturity. Height varies with site conditions.
- Only single-trunk trees with a clear central leader may be planted along streets. Multi-stemmed trees are allowed only in open areas such as parks.
- "Native" on this list is regional. It means the species was historically present in the Mid-Atlantic, not necessarily in Baltimore City itself.

## Eastern redbud (Cercis canadensis)
- On the list as an approved small street tree.
- Native status: **Native**.
- City note: **prone to structural problems**, meaning it may have weak wood or poor branch attachment and may need extra routine pruning.

## Serviceberry (Amelanchier canadensis)
- Listed as Canadian serviceberry, an approved small street tree.
- Native status: **Native**.
- Two related serviceberries are also approved as small street trees and listed as native: common serviceberry (Amelanchier arborea) and Allegheny serviceberry (Amelanchier laevis).

## Hedge maple (Acer campestre): not approved
The 2025 list places hedge maple under "Trees Not to be Planted Anywhere in the ROW or in Parks." It is marked **nonnative** and **invasive**. (An older 2018 city list had included it as a small tree for under power lines, so older guides may still recommend it.) The city says existing healthy trees on this list will not be removed just for being on it.

## Other native small street trees on the same list
Examples include white fringetree (Chionanthus virginicus, marked as expected to do better as the climate warms), cockspur hawthorn (thornless variety), Washington hawthorn, sweetbay magnolia, Chickasaw plum, blackhaw and rusty blackhaw.

Before planting any tree in the public right-of-way, you need permission from the Baltimore City Forestry Division.
