**Key takeaway:** New green space can make a neighborhood healthier and nicer, but it can also raise housing costs and push out the residents it was meant to help. Researchers call this "green gentrification." Their answer is greening that is "just green enough": shaped by residents, spread across many small sites, and paired with protections against displacement.

## The paradox
The paper, "Urban green space, public health, and environmental justice: The challenge of making cities 'just green enough'" by Jennifer Wolch, Jason Byrne and Joshua Newell, reviews research on parks and green space. It finds that green space is often unevenly spread, with more going to whiter, wealthier communities, so access to it is an environmental justice issue.

But fixing that gap has a catch the authors call the urban green space paradox. When a low-income neighborhood gets new green space, it becomes more desirable, housing costs can rise, and renters may be priced out. People who are displaced may end up in other neighborhoods with the same lack of green space. The same effect goes by several names: ecological, green, environmental or eco-gentrification. The authors note this is not new; some famous park projects, including Central Park, were designed partly to raise land values.

## "Just green enough"
The idea comes from Curran and Hamilton's 2012 study of Greenpoint, Brooklyn, where residents pushed for a creek cleanup that served the existing working-class community instead of an upscale waterfront. Wolch and colleagues suggest:
- **Let the community lead.** Design projects around what residents say they need, not standard design formulas.
- **Go small and spread out.** Many small, scattered greening projects spread benefits more evenly and are less likely to spark a real estate boom than one big showcase project.
- **Protect residents.** Pair greening with affordable housing, housing trust funds, rent stabilization, help for homeownership, shared-equity housing, and protections for local businesses and jobs.

## Why it matters for street trees
Street trees are a small, scattered kind of greening, which fits the "just green enough" idea. Still, Baltimore residents interviewed in one study worried that tree planting could be followed by gentrification (see the East Baltimore resident concerns document). Asking residents, and planning for housing stability, helps make sure the people who live on a block today are the ones who benefit from its shade.
