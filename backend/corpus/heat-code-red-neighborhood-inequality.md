**Key takeaway:** Baltimore's heat is not shared equally. The 2019 "Code Red" investigation found that the city's hottest areas, such as McElderry Park in East Baltimore, tend to have more poverty, shorter life expectancy and far fewer trees, and heat islands there can run about 8°F hotter than greener, less paved communities.

## About the series
"Code Red: Baltimore's Climate Divide" was published on September 3, 2019, by the University of Maryland's Howard Center for Investigative Journalism with Capital News Service, NPR and partners. This story was reported by Ian Round, Jazmin Conner, Jermaine Rowley and Sandy Banisky.

## Heat on the street
- Using a thermal camera on the 500 block of North Milton Street in **McElderry Park** on August 1, 2019, reporters recorded surfaces as hot as **163°F** while the air was **87°F**.
- Researchers from Portland State University and the Science Museum of Virginia mapped Baltimore's heat block by block on **August 29, 2018**. The story reports that heat-island areas can be about **8°F** hotter than places with more trees and less pavement.
- The hottest parts of the city had higher poverty and unemployment, more violent crime and shorter average life spans, based on 2017 neighborhood data from the Baltimore Neighborhood Indicators Alliance.

## Heat inside rowhouses
Reporters placed temperature and humidity sensors in East Baltimore homes during a July 2019 heat wave. In some homes the readings showed it was hotter inside than outside, including in an unairconditioned bedroom at night. Many residents said they did not know cooling centers existed, and some centers were closed on weekends.

## Trees
- Citywide tree canopy was **28%** in 2015, up from **27%** in 2007.
- Many East Baltimore neighborhoods had canopy of about **10%** in 2015, and one block at the edge of McElderry Park had about **8%**.

## Baltimore's heat is getting worse
- Average yearly temperatures in Baltimore have risen **more than 3°F** over the last century, nearly twice as much as the rest of the country.
- Without cuts in carbon emissions, researchers projected more than **37** days a year with a heat index of 100°F or more by mid-century, and **65** such days by the end of the century.
- Maryland recorded **134** heat-related deaths from 2012 to 2018. **37** of them (**28%**) were in Baltimore, which has only about **10%** of the state's population.
- By the end of July 2019, the health department had declared **12** Code Red days that year and counted **2,454** people who had visited cooling centers.
