**Key takeaway:** Cities are hotter than the areas around them because pavement, roofs and buildings soak up and hold the sun's heat, and there are fewer trees to cool things down. EPA says U.S. urban areas run about 1–7°F warmer than outlying areas during the day and about 2–5°F warmer at night.

## What a heat island is
A heat island is a built-up area that is noticeably warmer than its surroundings. Roads, buildings and other structures absorb the sun's energy and give it back off as heat, much more than forests, fields or water do. Places with lots of pavement and few trees, like many Baltimore rowhouse blocks, become "islands" of higher temperature.

## Why it happens
- **Heat-absorbing materials:** Common materials like asphalt and conventional roofing reflect less sunlight and absorb more heat than natural ground. On a warm day, EPA notes a conventional roof can be as much as **66°F** hotter than the surrounding air.
- **Missing vegetation:** Trees, plants and water cool the air through shade and by releasing water vapor. Hard surfaces cannot do this.
- **City shape:** Narrow streets lined with tall buildings form "urban canyons" that block breezes and trap heat.
- **Waste heat:** Cars, air conditioners and factories release extra heat into the city.

## Surface heat vs. air heat
EPA separates two kinds of heat islands. **Surface heat islands** are hot ground and roofs, the kind you feel when you touch a sidewalk or see in satellite images. **Atmospheric (air) heat islands** are warmer air, which is what people breathe and feel; this effect is usually less extreme than surface heat.

## Why it matters
- **Energy:** Demand for electricity to run air conditioning rises about **1–9%** for every **2°F** increase in temperature.
- **Health:** Hotter cities mean more heat illness and death. EPA cites an average of **702** heat deaths per year in the U.S. (2004–2018).
- **Air and water:** More power use means more air pollution and greenhouse gases, and hot runoff from pavement can warm urban streams by as much as **18°F**.
