**Key takeaway:** A global review of 182 studies found that urban trees can lower temperatures at street level by as much as 12°C, mostly by blocking sunlight and releasing water vapor. How much cooling trees provide depends on the local climate, the shape of the city, and the kinds of trees planted.

## The study
Haiwei Li, Yongling Zhao, Chenghao Wang, Diana Ürge-Vorsatz, Jan Carmeliet and Ronita Bardhan pulled together results from **182** published studies on tree cooling, covering **17** climate types in **110** cities or regions around the world. It was published in Communications Earth & Environment in December 2024. This kind of study, which combines many earlier studies, is often called a meta-analysis.

## What they found
- **Up to 12°C cooler:** Trees can lower pedestrian-level temperatures by up to **12°C**, through shade (blocking radiation) and transpiration (releasing water from leaves). This is a maximum, not a typical value.
- **Keeping peaks in check:** Planting trees brought peak monthly temperatures below **26°C** in **83%** of the cities studied.
- **Mixing tree types helps:** In tropical, temperate and continental climates, combining deciduous trees (which drop leaves in winter) with evergreens in open, less crowded city layouts gave about **0.5°C** more cooling than planting only one species.
- **Dry cities differ:** In arid climates, evergreen trees in compact, dense layouts tended to cool best.
- **Trees are not a cure-all:** The authors note that trees are often seen as the complete answer to urban heat, but their cooling varies a lot from place to place.

## What it means for Baltimore
Baltimore has a humid climate with hot summers, so the findings for temperate climates are the most relevant: a mix of tree species along open streets is likely to cool better than a single kind of tree. Real cooling on any one block will usually be far less than the 12°C maximum and depends on how much shade the trees actually cast.
