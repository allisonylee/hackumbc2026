**Key takeaway:** American hornbeam and American hophornbeam are native medium-size street trees on Baltimore's current approved list. Kentucky yellowwood is also approved as a medium street tree, but the city lists it as native to the **Southeast** US, not the Mid-Atlantic.

## How the city defines "medium"
- **Medium trees:** 35 to 50 feet tall at maturity. Height varies with site conditions.
- Street locations include tree pits and grass strips. The city notes that large shade trees give more benefits and live longer than small understory trees, so it prefers larger trees wherever there is room.
- "Native" on this list means the species was historically present in the Mid-Atlantic region. Trees native to other parts of the US are labeled by region (for example "Southeast").

## American hornbeam (Carpinus caroliniana)
- Approved medium street tree.
- Native status: **Native**.
- Don't confuse it with European hornbeam (Carpinus betulus), which is also approved but is **nonnative**.

## American hophornbeam (Ostrya virginiana)
- Listed simply as "hophornbeam," an approved medium street tree.
- Native status: **Native**.

## Kentucky yellowwood (Cladrastis kentukea)
- Approved medium street tree.
- Native status: **Southeast**. It is native to the US but not historically to the Mid-Atlantic, so it does not count toward the city's rule that at least half the trees in a planting project be native.

## Other medium street trees on the same list
Native options include river birch (single trunk only on streets), sugarberry (marked as expected to do better in a warmer climate), common hackberry and 'Purple Robe' black locust. Nonnative options include katsura, Chinese pistache, Sargent cherry and Regal Prince oak (a columnar form).

Before planting any tree in the public right-of-way, you need permission from the Baltimore City Forestry Division.
