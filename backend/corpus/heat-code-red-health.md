**Key takeaway:** In Baltimore, emergency medical calls for many heat-sensitive conditions jumped when the heat index rose above 103°F, according to a Howard Center data analysis. Older adults, children, pregnant women and people with chronic illnesses were most at risk, and several East Baltimore homes that reporters monitored lacked enough air conditioning.

## About the story
This story, "Health risks rise with the temperature," is part of the Code Red series published September 3, 2019, by the University of Maryland's Howard Center for Investigative Journalism and Capital News Service. It was reported by Ian Round, Jazmin Conner, Jermaine Rowley and John Fairhall.

## The 103°F threshold
When the heat index went above **103°F**, Baltimore emergency medical calls spiked for:
- dehydration and respiratory distress
- kidney disease and diabetes complications
- heart attacks and heart failure
- psychiatric disorders and drug and alcohol overdoses

Several days in a row at a heat index of 103°F or more are especially dangerous for older people, children, pregnant women and anyone with a chronic disease that heat makes worse. An emergency doctor at The Johns Hopkins Hospital said patients tend to need longer hospital stays when temperatures soar.

## Inside hot homes
Reporters placed sensors in homes in **Broadway East** and **McElderry Park** during a July 2019 heat wave that included two 100°F days and a Code Red heat emergency.
- In one Broadway East home with no air conditioning, the indoor heat index reached **116°F** on July 18.
- In a McElderry Park bedroom, the heat index climbed as high as **118°F** and never fell below **88°F** over seven days.

Residents described conditions like asthma, diabetes, high blood pressure and heart disease that got worse in the heat. Air conditioning helps keep asthma under control, the story notes, but one McElderry Park mother of three sons with asthma could only cool part of the house.
