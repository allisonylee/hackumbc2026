**Key takeaway:** On one of the hottest afternoons of summer 2018, a volunteer mapping project found parts of Baltimore reached 103°F while the coolest parts of the city were about 17°F cooler at the same time.

## What was measured
In summer 2018, NOAA supported a volunteer "citizen science" project to make very detailed maps of the urban heat island effect in Baltimore and Washington, DC. Volunteers recorded temperatures across each city on one of the hottest days of the summer, in the afternoon. NOAA's Climate.gov team then turned the data into maps and placed them next to matching satellite images, so people could compare hot spots with what is on the ground (buildings, pavement, trees, water). The feature was published on October 15, 2018.

## The temperature spread
- **Baltimore:** some neighborhoods reached **103°F**, roughly **17°F** hotter than the coolest parts of the city at the same time of day.
- **Washington, DC** (one day earlier): several neighborhoods reached **102°F**, while other parts of the city were about **16°F** cooler.

That means two people in the same city, on the same afternoon, can experience very different heat depending on where they live.

## Where it was hottest
The hottest areas were in the center of the Baltimore metro area, where development is densest, with warm zones stretching south on both sides of the Patapsco River. Places both inside and outside the Beltway, including **Pimlico**, **Towson** and **Nottingham**, showed up as warm spots. They were linked back to the hot urban core by thin "ribbons" of warmth that follow major roads.

## Why it matters
NOAA and its partners shared the maps with officials in Baltimore and DC, who were interested in using them to plan cooling strategies and improve resilience to heat. NOAA was also developing a way to run similar mapping campaigns in many more U.S. cities in 2019 and beyond. Maps like these help show which blocks would benefit most from shade and other cooling measures.
