**Key takeaway:** Hot weather can make asthma worse, both directly and by making the air dirtier. Heat helps form ground-level ozone, and CDC notes that breathing unhealthy ozone levels for as little as one day can trigger asthma attacks. Children and teens are especially vulnerable.

## How heat worsens asthma
- **Dehydration:** Heat can dry out the body, which can make asthma symptoms more severe.
- **Humidity:** Muggy air can make it harder for the lungs to work well.
- **Ozone and pollution:** Higher temperatures speed up the formation of ground-level ozone and can raise levels of particle pollution and sulfur dioxide, all of which irritate the airways.
- **Pollen and smoke:** Warmer weather can stretch pollen season, which has grown by several weeks in many U.S. regions, and raises the risk of wildfires, whose smoke is another trigger.
- **Several triggers at once:** Smoke, pollen and mold (for example after flooding) can pile up at the same time.

## Why children are at higher risk
Children react differently to heat than adults, know less about its dangers, and are less able to get themselves out of a hot place. Kids with weaker lung function, obesity or other health conditions face even more risk.

## Practical steps CDC recommends
- **Check the Air Quality Index (AQI) daily.** When it is **above 100**, outdoor air is considered unhealthy for sensitive groups, including children and teens with asthma.
- **Check the HeatRisk forecast.** Most children should take protective steps at the Orange level, and some need to act at Yellow.
- **Know the signs of overheating,** such as trouble breathing or getting more tired than usual during exercise.
- **Protect inhalers.** Keep them out of hot cars and other hot places; high heat can damage them.
- **Review medicines** with a doctor, since some can increase heat sensitivity.
- **Make a heat action plan** with your child's health care provider.
