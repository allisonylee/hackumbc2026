---
title: "Why do large trees give more benefits than small trees? (plant the biggest tree the space allows)"
url: https://www.itreetools.org/documents/443/PSW_GTR202_Northeast_CTG.pdf
source: McPherson et al., USDA Forest Service, Northeast Community Tree Guide (PSW-GTR-202), 2007
---

**Key takeaway:** Most of what a tree does for a city (shade, catching rain, filtering air) depends on how much leaf area it has. Large-stature trees grow far more leaves, so where there is room, planting a large tree instead of a small one multiplies the benefits many times over.

## Leaf area drives the benefits
The US Forest Service Northeast guide explains that summer shade, rainfall interception and pollutant uptake are all tied to leaf surface area. Twenty years after planting, its model trees had:
- Small (Kwanzan cherry): 17 ft tall, 338 sq ft of leaf area
- Medium (red maple): 29 ft tall, 1,725 sq ft of leaf area
- Large (Japanese zelkova): 38 ft tall, 2,510 sq ft of leaf area

Averaged over 40 years, the large tree intercepted about 1,909 gallons of rain per year versus 358 gallons for the small tree. Air quality benefits ranged from $3 to $13 per tree per year, with the large tree highest. The guide's summary figure notes that large trees store more carbon, filter more air pollution, catch more rain and save more energy than small trees.

## The dollar gap
Average yearly net benefit for a public tree was $9 (small), $52 (medium) and $113 (large). Forty years after planting, a large public tree was giving about $154 per year in net benefits compared with $27 for a small one. Large trees do cost more to care for (about $34 vs $20 per year for public trees), mainly for pruning and eventual removal, but the benefits grow much faster than the costs.

## A worked example from the guide
In a hypothetical city planting 500 each of small, medium and large street trees, net benefits over 40 years came to $262 per small tree, $1,923 per medium tree and $4,321 per large tree. The guide estimated that planting only small trees would give up about $6.8 million in benefits, far more than the $675,000 the city would save on planting. The city kept its policy of planting large trees where space allows.

## When small trees are the right choice
The guide still recommends small trees (under 25 ft tall) under overhead power lines and in small planting sites. Large trees under wires need heavy, repeated pruning, which raises costs and cuts their benefits. The guide also warns against relying on too few species, since one pest can then wipe out many trees at once.
